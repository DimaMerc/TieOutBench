HEADLINE (goes in the article title field; 84 characters):

An AI checker reviewed the work of eight AI models. Fewer runs passed every check.


BODY (paste below into the article editor; formatting notes at the bottom):

Latest in a series on building runnable evaluations for finance LLMs. The last one gave eight AI models every document for a corporate action in one prompt. This one gives the same models the tools a desk uses, and then adds the control that operations already relies on: a second reviewer. The tools raised the number of runs that passed every check. The reviewer lowered it. Part of the reason is in the reviewers, and part of it is in the environment I built.


Two objections to the last test

In September I published the results of eight AI models on six corporate-actions cases. One small model found the corrected dividend notice, stated the new record date, and then booked USD 8,500 where USD 6,800 was due. It had counted the shares as of the old record date.

Two objections to that test are fair. The model had every document in a single prompt and nothing to look anything up with. And nobody checked its work before it was graded. A desk has a position system to ask, and a second person who reviews a booking before it is released.

This evaluation tests both, on the same six cases, with the same eight models and the same grader.


The setup

The cases are unchanged: four real events, cited to the issuers' filings. The accounts, the positions and the ETF basket are constructed, and every case file says so.

What changed is how the model works. It no longer receives the documents. It receives tools: list the documents in the store and read them, ask for the account's position as of any date, calculate, and book. A booking is an entry in a test ledger: a receivable, a basket update, a position confirmation, an election, or an escalation that names a missing document and holds the one action that depends on it. No payment is made anywhere.

The grader reads two things: the worksheet the model submits, and the ledger. A worksheet that says 6,800 over a ledger that booked 8,500 fails. Which documents the model read, and which dates it asked for, are recorded and reported. They are not scored.

I ran this four ways, 48 runs each, one run per model per case.

Prompt only. These are the runs from the last article.

Tools.

Tools and a checker. A second agent of the same model receives the task, the worksheet and the ledger, but not the first model's conversation. It can read the documents, query positions and calculate. It approves or rejects. After a rejection the bookings are voided and the first model has one round to revise.

Tools and a stronger checker. The same, with one flagship model reviewing the work of all eight.


Tools helped

Runs that passed every check: 32 with the prompt only, 35 with tools. In the 48 tools runs, no model booked an amount on superseded terms.

The recorded calls show how the models worked. All 48 runs read the governing document before the first booking. 46 asked for the position at least once. 37 asked for it as of the date the entitlement is based on. On the corrected dividend, five models asked for the position on both record dates, saw 50,000 shares on the old one and 40,000 on the new one, and booked on 40,000.

Then I looked again at the small model from the last article. I have five saved attempts of it on the corrected dividend with tools. In two it booked USD 8,500. Both times it read the correction, read a position report, called the calculator with 50,000 shares, and never asked the position tool. In three it booked USD 6,800. Each time it asked for the position as of the desk's date, not the record date. The amount was right because nothing changed between those two dates. The note on one of the wrong bookings reads: "account position as of governing record date 2024-08-23 not separately provided, so booked on store-determined entitlement basis from available position history and corrected terms."

The tool that answers that question was available in all five attempts. One disclosure: these are five saved attempts, not five identical trials. The first stopped on an error in my harness after the booking was saved, and I changed the harness between attempts.

So tools helped, and access to the data was not the control. The control would be a check that the position was requested as of the record date before anything is booked.


The checker lowered the result

With a checker of the same model, 27 runs passed every check, against 35 without one.

The reviewers looked things up. All 48 queried the position in their first round, and 45 recalculated. Then they rejected 20 of the 46 ledgers my grader scores as correct, and approved one of the two it scores as wrong. Every one of the 21 rejections was followed by a revision.

I read the 20 rejections finding by finding. Thirteen raise nothing the documents support. One reviewer asked for the entitlement date of the stock split to be replaced with the record date of a dividend, which is a different event. The maker made the change, and its score fell from 1.000 to 0.475. Several reviewers told the maker that net cash equals gross cash because no document mentions withholding, and the maker replaced a correct refusal with a number. One review contains two findings, both of which say the entries are right, and its verdict is reject.

Seven raise at least one fair point, and five of those are the same point. My booking tool requires a payment date. No document in the store states the payment date of the tender. The makers entered the date of the results announcement, and the reviewers objected, correctly, that the date has no source. One wrote: "The booked pay_date of 2024-06-10 is unsupported." My grader scores the amount and the share count of that booking, not its date, which is why those ledgers count as correct. Three makers answered the objection by removing the booking. A correct receivable went missing from the ledger because my tool cannot book without a date.

Of the eight runs the checker cost, five trace to reviewer findings the documents do not support, and three to that payment date.


A stronger checker did not fix it

With one flagship model reviewing all eight makers, 25 runs passed every check.

Its verdicts were better. It rejected 13 ledgers that the grader scores as correct, against 20. It also found an error the same-model reviewers had missed: a position confirmed at 180,000 shares as of the split's record date, a day before the new shares were distributed.

But ten of its rejections told the maker to replace a refusal with a value. Nine were about tax. In the reviewer's words, about the constructed fund in the case: "MEGA is a U.S. ETF and NVIDIA Corporation is a U.S. issuer; a U.S. fund's dividend from a U.S. corporation is not subject to withholding." One was about fees on the tender. The documents state neither the fund's tax status nor the fees. In all ten runs the maker entered the value. Ten answers that had correctly declined to state an amount now stated one. The reviewer was not consistent about it: on the same tax question it over-ruled the refusal in nine runs and approved it in five.

The counter-argument belongs here. The reviewer's tax reasoning is plausible: a U.S. fund ordinarily has no withholding on a dividend from a U.S. company. The case is built on the rule the makers were given: if the documents do not determine the answer, say so, name the missing document, and hold only the figure that depends on it. The reviewer was never given that rule. Its instruction was to tie the work out to the source and approve only what ties out.

That qualification applies to both checker results. The reviewers had the same documents and the same tools as the makers. They did not have the same rules.


What I conclude, and what I do not

In these runs, one round of AI review made the graded result worse, with a checker of the same model and with a stronger one. In 20 of the 21 revisions the maker changed its bookings.

A checker needs the rules, not only the documents. Without them, the stronger reviewer used what it knew about tax to fill the gap, and six of the eight models wrote it down.

The environment is part of the control. A required field with no source, and a label that reads like an error, produced objections that had nothing to do with the quality of the work.

I do not conclude that AI review does not work. This was one protocol: one revision round, a reviewer with full authority, and makers that did not check a finding against the source before acting on it. I do not conclude that a person would have done better as the checker. That was not tested. And these counts are recorded results, not rates: one run per model per case, with three repeats on one case.

The repeats showed both kinds of result. One mid-tier model passed every check in all twelve of its repeat runs, across the four ways of working. And the same-model reviewer of another mid-tier model gave three verdicts on three correct ledgers with the same bookings: approve, reject, approve.


Testing my own test

The first time I ran the checker, I had not given the reviewer the question the maker had been asked about the missing document. The reviewers rejected correct escalations as unsupported. I fixed what the reviewer receives and ran the whole arm again. The first wave is kept in the repository, and every number in this article is from the second.

The new runs also found eight places where my grader marked a correct answer wrong. Each is fixed and has a regression check. No published score or gate moved. One diagnostic sub-score did, and the repository says which.

Before writing this, I read every count in the repository's documents against the saved runs and corrected what did not match, including a sentence that said all ten of the stronger reviewer's over-rules were about tax. Nine were.


What is next

The next run gives the checker the desk's rules. It may reject a booking. It may not tell a maker to replace a refusal with a value. And the maker checks each finding against the source before it changes anything.

The cases, the grader, every transcript, every ledger and every verdict are open in the repository (github.com/DimaMerc/TieOutBench), and the technical report for the first five evaluations is on SSRN.

If you run a maker-checker control: what does your checker receive that the maker does not?
