HEADLINE (goes in the article title field):

I ran eight frontier models through three finance workflows. The first three things I found were bugs in my own harness.


BODY (paste below into the article editor; formatting notes at the bottom):

Part of a series on building runnable evaluations for finance LLMs. Until this summer, every eval I had published carried the same caveat: the models I tested all came from one vendor. This is what happened when I fixed that, and why the fix mattered more than the scores.

For six weeks, every result I published came with an asterisk. The evals were real, the gold answers were cited, the gates fired where they should. But every frontier model I had run came from a single vendor. I wrote that caveat into the README, the leaderboard, and the bottom of every article, because a benchmark that has only met one family of models has not been tested.

So I ran two more vendors: eight models in total - three from Anthropic, four from OpenAI, one from Google - across three of the workflows, on the same gold cases, with the same rubrics and the same auto-fail gates.

The findings come later, and the full model-by-model grid is in the repo, linked at the end. I start with what went wrong, because it is the more useful part.


Three breakages before a single comparable number

Adding a second vendor to a working harness should be a configuration change. It was not. The harness broke three times, and each time the output looked like a result.

The first break was visible and harmless. OpenAI's newest models reject the field every other OpenAI-compatible API uses to cap response length; they require a different field name. Every call failed with an error. I taught the client to swap the field and retry.

The second break produced no error. Modern models "think" before they answer, and the thinking costs tokens. The endpoints disagree about who pays for it. On the endpoint I had been using, thinking did not count against the limit I set on the answer. On OpenAI's, it does: reasoning tokens come out of the same budget as the response.

So the budget I had been using was too small for the new models on my longest case, a discounted-cash-flow valuation with a prompt of about 4,400 tokens. The models spent the budget thinking and had little or nothing left for the answer.

This is what that looked like on the scoreboard, before and after I gave every model equivalent room:

- One model returned nothing at all. An empty response, scored as a failure.
- Another scored 0.322 and tripped six auto-fail gates at once.
- A third scored 0.618 and tripped the false-precision gate.
- A fourth scored 0.745.

With a proper budget, the same four models scored 0.953, 0.951, 0.903, and 0.631. All four original numbers were wrong. Three understated the model badly: the one that "failed six gates" was, given room to finish, one of the strongest performers in the study. The fourth moved the other way, and its gate turned out to be real.

Nothing crashed. Nothing logged a warning. I had four plausible scores that told a clean story: the new vendor is worse at valuation, and its weakest model is very bad. Every one of those numbers measured my configuration, not the models. Six gates firing at once is not a model making six mistakes. It is a sentence cut off before the answer.

The third break was the hardest to see, and I only looked because of the second one.

Google's endpoint also charges thinking against the answer budget, and does not report it. I sent a one-line test question. The response said it used three tokens on the answer and eight on my question, and that the total was 124. The missing 113 tokens went to thinking, and they appear in no field a harness would normally read.

If I had sized budgets from the field that says "tokens used in the completion", I would have starved that model on every long case, and the symptom would have been low scores rather than an error. I would not have found that bug by reading results.

Three endpoints, three different answers to who pays for thinking and whether you are told. None of them documented where I was looking. (Each vendor's native API behaves differently again. These are the compatibility layers, which is what a harness like mine talks to.)


The rule this produces

You cannot compare models across vendors until you have shown that your harness gives each of them equivalent room to work.

That reads as obvious. In practice it is not, because the failure does not announce itself. A starved model does not return an error; it returns a worse answer. A worse answer is what you are looking for when you benchmark, so you record it and move on.

This is the same trap the series is about, one level up. My evals exist because a model's answer can look right and be wrong, so I grade every step instead of the final number. An evaluation can look right and be wrong in the same way. I had seen a version of this once before, on a single model, and treated it as a one-off. It took a second vendor to show it was a rule. A benchmark that has met one family of models has not been tested.


The findings

With every model given equivalent room, this is what eight models across three vendors show.

The most reproducible result in the suite got stronger. Across all eight models, on both back-office workflows, no model made the catastrophic decision. None affirmed a swap confirmation whose economic terms did not tie. None settled a creation basket that was short by thirteen thousand dollars. None raised a false alarm on the clean control cases. With three models from one vendor I called this a promising negative result. With eight models across three vendors it looks like a property of the task: frontier models get the go/no-go decision right and lose their points on the arithmetic underneath it. (Since these runs, the small tier has given me one exception, on a corporate action. That is a separate piece later this month.)

The top tiers have converged. The three flagship models sit within four hundredths of each other on every case, and within a hundredth and a half on all but one. On the confirmation case with a real break in it, five of the eight tie at the same score. On this task, at this difficulty, the eval no longer separates the frontier. That is a finding about my eval as much as about the models. The response is to make the task harder, not to read the ranking as meaningful.

Cheap stopped predicting bad. Two vendors' small, fast models fail here, and they fail differently. One fails on arithmetic: it overstates a basket by two hundred thousand dollars, which turns a shortfall into an apparent surplus. The other fails on calibration: it states a fair value of $200.20 per share, to the cent, and leaves the growth-rate sensitivity blank on a valuation that is roughly eighty percent terminal value. That is the false-precision gate, which exists for that case.

The third vendor's small, fast model fired no gate anywhere, tied the best score on the broken-basket case, and landed its valuation at $227.81 against a gold answer of $227.82, with sensitivity ranges on both drivers.

Two data points said small models are undeployable on this work. The third says that was a coincidence of two vendors. Price tier and trustworthiness are different axes. You have to measure.

One more detail, the kind only step-by-step grading catches. On the reconciliation case, every model faces a deliberate trap: I ask for a figure that cannot be determined from the documents provided. The correct answer is to refuse and say what is missing. All eight refused correctly, with the right label and no invented number. But one of them, where it was supposed to explain what was missing, echoed my own instruction back almost word for word. It also got a related, answerable figure wrong by twenty thousand dollars. Graded on the refusal alone, that model looks perfectly calibrated.


Why this matters beyond my repo

In April, US banking regulators rewrote model-risk guidance and left generative AI out of scope. I wrote about that gap in July: firms deploying AI into real financial workflows now have to define for themselves what "validated" means.

This study is a small, concrete example of what that takes. Three vendors, three undocumented differences in how a request is metered, each able to produce a confident wrong number about a model's competence. None of them was visible in the outputs. All of them were visible once the comparison was fair.

If your firm is benchmarking models to decide what to deploy, the first question is not which model won. It is whether your harness gave them the same test. Mine did not, twice, and I built it.

One caveat: this is one run per model per case. That is enough to expose a broken harness and to say whether a gate fired. It is not enough to rank models by hundredths. Read the small gaps as "indistinguishable here", not as a ranking.

Everything here is runnable and MIT-licensed: the workflows, the rubrics with every gate, the gold cases, all graded runs from all eight models, and the leaderboard with its caveats. Two commands reproduce the core with no API key.

Leaderboard and repo (the suite is called TieOutBench): evals.finance
