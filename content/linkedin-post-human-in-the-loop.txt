Post-trade AI: trust the call, check the number.

Since my Munich post, many of you reading this are working in custody, ETF servicing and post-trade - in Frankfurt, Zurich, Vienna. Here is something I keep noticing about AI in fund operations, and I'd like your view.

I went through as many public statements as I could find from custodians, CSDs, vendors and regulators about AI in operations. Nearly all of them promise the same thing: a human in the loop, and everything auditable.

The same firms are putting agents on work like reconciliation, payment validation and corporate actions - and reporting lower headcount in the same breath. ESMA's survey of 728 firms found human oversight is the most-cited safeguard, at 65%.

Almost none of them say where the human sits. The few that do put the human on the decision - the final call, the sign-off, the attestation.

In my own evaluation work, I run frontier models through back-office tasks built from real filings and real trade formats: reconcile an ETF creation basket, match a swap confirmation, build a valuation. Eight models, three vendors, one run per model per case. In every one of those runs, the strong models got the go/no-go decision right. None settled the broken basket. None affirmed the mismatched trade. That was the strong tier. The cheapest tier has since given me one exception, on a corporate action - a post of its own.

Where they slip is in the numbers under the decision. One example: two confirmations of the same EUR 50 million swap, fixed rates 5 basis points apart - about EUR 25,000 a year. Every model returned mismatch, do not affirm.

But when asked how big the problem was, one model said 0.5 basis points - about EUR 2,500 a year. Another said 50 basis points - which on that swap is about EUR 250,000 a year. The first would be ignored as noise. The second would trigger an escalation. Right call, wrong size, in both directions.

In fund accounting the check that catches this has a name: the tie-out. Before a number is released, someone confirms it agrees with the number it came from. The basket to the file it was built from. The confirmation to the terms. A number that does not tie out does not settle.

That is where I would put the human. A review that only repeats the decision is checking the part that held in every one of my runs, and missing the part that slipped. With a strong model, put the human on the tie-out.

How does your desk decide where the human sits? I suspect the Zurich answer and the Boston answer are not the same, and I'd like to hear both.
