One leg of an ETF redemption moved at 12,000 option contracts. 120 were due. The agent's own report said all four legs were fine.

Settle, or hold?

The step is one the clearing house itself calls manual and error-prone: when a fund made of listed options is redeemed, the option positions have to move from the fund's account to the dealer's, and someone has to confirm that what moved is what was due. I built the case so that the share count landed where the contract count belonged, and gave it to eight paid AI models, together with a clean twin where every leg ties.

- All eight found the wrong leg, the 12,000 against 120, and the 11,880 contracts that have to come back. None settled. All eight said the return comes first and named both clearing firms.
- All eight settled the clean twin. Nobody held an order that was right.
- Nine of sixteen runs passed every check. Three models were perfect on both.
- Nobody guessed what the dealer had paid for its shares, which the documents do not say.

What went wrong was one layer down, in the arithmetic. One small model valued the options per share instead of per contract, so every leg came out at one hundredth of its value; the test has a check built for exactly that, and it fired. One mid-size model put the excess at ten times its value and carried the error into its hold note. The decisions were right every time; the sizes were not.

Before any model ran, six AI reviewers spent a day trying to fool my grader. They found a lot. All of it is fixed and published, with the before-and-after.

This is the eighth finance task I have put to AI this way: one task at a time, every answer checked against the source documents, all results published.
