I ran eight AI models on a corrected dividend. One of them read it exactly the way a careful analyst would - and booked the wrong number.

It found the correction, not the original announcement. It listed the original as superseded, in its own worksheet. It stated the corrected record date.

Then it counted the shares from the original record date, and booked USD 8,500 against the correct payment date. The right amount was USD 6,800.

If you only read its explanation, you would approve it.

I have now run eight frontier models through six cases built on four real corporate actions - a stock split inside an ETF basket, an oversubscribed tender with an odd-lot rule, two corrected dividends - forty-eight runs in all. Two things happened that I did not expect.

Thirty-two runs passed every check with nothing to flag. One mid-tier model passed all six cases perfectly - the first perfect score from a live model in this series.

And the least expensive model of the eight did what I described above, which is the exact failure I built the evaluation to catch: the right explanation wrapped around the wrong number, and the payment booked.

Where the human review belongs is a question I keep coming back to, and this changed my answer a little. The article has the two smaller failures that repeated across models, what it took to make the grader trustworthy, and where I would put the human now. Link in the first comment.
