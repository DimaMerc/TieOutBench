AI doesn't just make right answers faster. It makes wrong ones more convincing.

That is the pattern behind a technical report I put on SSRN this summer, and the reason it exists.

This year I built runnable evaluations of real finance work - earnings analysis, buffer-ETF diligence, DCF valuation, and two back-office controls almost nobody outside operations has heard of: reconciling an ETF creation basket, and matching both parties' confirmations of a swap. Every gold figure is cited to a source document or labeled as constructed, and auto-fail gates catch the mistakes that quietly poison a memo. Eight frontier models from three vendors ran the valuation and the two back-office controls, and every run is published.

In those runs the models got the decision right and lost their points on the number underneath - and the wrong number arrived with a fluent, well-structured explanation.

The valuation case shows how that hides. Skip the net-debt bridge and the answer lands within 3 percent of the market price: the wrong method produces the plausible number. On the reconciliation case, a basket that was 13,320 dollars short was correctly refused - with arithmetic that was 200,000 dollars off.

None of that shows up in a blended accuracy score. It only shows up when you grade each step and let the auto-fail gates trigger.

Why it matters beyond my repository: this summer I looked at as many public statements as I could find about AI in post-trade operations. In the largest industry survey I found, 57 percent of firms say they are piloting generative AI there. Nearly every vendor page promises a human in the loop. Across some twenty-five vendor pages, not one cited an independent measurement of how often the AI is right. Faster is measured. Right is asserted.

The report (TieOutBench) is my attempt at an independent measurement: the method, the grid, and the failures - including the ones in my own harness. Link in the first comment. If you validate models for a living, or you are the person who has to sign off on one, I would like to know where it falls short.
